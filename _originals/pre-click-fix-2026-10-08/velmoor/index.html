Chapter One

The Iron Gates

The gates were iron and unreasonable.

Seraphine Vale had known, in the abstract, that Velmoor Academy would be large. She had read the prospectus three times on the overnight coach — folding and unfolding it until the paper softened at the creases — and the illustrations had been modest watercolours, tastefully composed, the kind of images designed to suggest tradition rather than size. What the watercolours had not communicated, and what she understood now with her case set down on the road and her neck tilted back at an angle that was going to ache tomorrow, was the sheer vertical fact of the place.

The main gates stood at the end of a half-mile approach road lined with elm trees, their canopies meeting overhead in a vault of amber and dying gold. It was the last week of September, and the light was doing the particular thing it did in autumn at dusk: turning everything briefly, mercilessly beautiful before the dark came down. Velmoor's towers caught it. The stone — pale grey, shot through with something that might have been quartz or might have been something older — seemed almost to generate its own illumination, the way certain things do when the angle is right and the viewer is tired and has been on a coach since before sunrise.

Seraphine picked up her case and walked through the gates.

Other students had not walked. She had passed three carriages on the approach road, the horses stepping high and unhurried, crests on the doors she didn't recognise but instinctively understood. Old crests. The kind that had been painted and repainted across generations until the original animal was more suggestion than image. The students inside the carriages had not looked at her. She had noted that too.

The gates were engraved. She almost missed it — the script was small, worked into the ironwork between climbing vines that might have been decorative or might have been structural, it was impossible to tell. She stopped and read it.

Wisdom is the inheritance of those who seek her.

She stood with the words for a moment. Then she walked on.
